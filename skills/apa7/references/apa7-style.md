# APA 7 Writing Style Catalog

The prose half of the audit. `apa7-rules.md` covers how a paper *looks*; this file
covers how it *reads*: Chapters 1, 4, 5, 8 (and the emphasis/abbreviation bits of 6)
of the *Publication Manual* (7th ed., 2020). Paraphrased checklists; section numbers
match the manual so findings can cite them (e.g. "§4.5 Wordiness").

**Severity tags.** `[hard]`/`[HARD]` = the manual states a rule → report as an error.
`[judgment]`/`[J]` = the manual recommends → report as a suggestion, never an error.
Items under "Beyond the 2020 manual" are newer guidance: mention only, never flag.

## A. Scholarly voice, paper types, and crediting sources

Source: *Publication Manual*, 7th ed. (2020). All wording below is paraphrase; examples are invented.
Scope: the craft of crediting and integrating sources. Citation *format* (et al., block-quote threshold, reference templates) lives elsewhere in the skill.
Legend: **[J]** = judgment call, report as a suggestion, not an error. Unmarked = report as an issue.

### 1.10 Student papers, dissertations, and theses (pp. 10–12)

- **Instructor rules win.** APA sets no formal requirements for student-paper length, required sections, or content. Before flagging structure, ask for (or check) the assignment prompt or rubric; it overrides the manual. **[J]**
- **Identify the paper type first**, because expectations differ. The manual's student types:
  1. **Annotated bibliography**: reference entries, each followed by a short annotation.
  2. **Cause-and-effect essay**: a clear thesis, then each cause paired with its effect, linked by transitions.
  3. **Comparative essay**: two or more items tied together by one central thesis; organized topic-by-topic or interwoven.
  4. **Expository essay**: introduction, body, conclusion; claims backed by evidence.
  5. **Narrative essay**: a story from a clear point of view, with a defined purpose and concise, vivid language.
  6. **Persuasive (argumentative) essay**: clear arguments and logical transitions aimed at moving the reader to a view or action.
  7. **Précis**: a short summary, in the student's own words, of a single work. It runs about a quarter of the original's length and mirrors its sections.
  8. **Response/reaction (reflection) paper**: summarizes a work, then gives the writer's personal reaction. It is usually short, and first person is expected.
  9. **Literature review** (see 1.6): the same genre as the published article.
- Flag **a thesis-driven type with no identifiable thesis** (cause-and-effect, comparative, persuasive, expository). **[J]**
  - Wrong: "This paper will discuss ADHD and intelligence testing."
  - Right: "WAIS processing-speed scores underestimate ability in adults with ADHD, so they should be read alongside index-level discrepancies."
- Flag **a précis that adds the writer's opinion**, or one far longer than about a quarter of the source. **[J]**
- Flag **a reflection or response paper that avoids "I"** through contortions such as "this writer felt" or "the author of this paper." First person is correct there (see 4.16).
- Flag **a reflection that only summarizes** and gives no personal reaction or relevance. **[J]**
- Flag **a comparative essay that treats each item in isolation** and never draws the comparison. **[J]**

### 1.6 Literature review articles (p. 8)

- Flag **a review that lists study after study without synthesis.** A review should define the problem, summarize prior work, identify relations, contradictions, and gaps, and suggest next steps. **[J]**
  - Wrong: "Smith (2019) found X. Jones (2020) found Y. Lee (2021) found Z."
  - Right: "Three studies converge on X (Jones, 2020; Lee, 2021; Smith, 2019), but none sampled adults over 65."
- Flag **a review with no organizing principle** (chronological, conceptual, or methodological) that the reader can follow. **[J]**

### 1.16–1.17 Duplicate publication, plagiarism, self-plagiarism (pp. 17–21)

- Flag **reused prior coursework** (the student's own earlier paper submitted again) unless the writer notes instructor permission. Many schools treat this as self-plagiarism. **[J]**: ask the writer; don't accuse.
- Building a thesis or dissertation from earlier classwork can be acceptable. Recommend that the writer confirm with the advisor and the honor code. **[J]**

### 8.1 Appropriate level of citation (pp. 253–254)

- **Undercitation:** flag facts, figures, statistics, theories, and definitions that are not common knowledge and carry no citation.
  - Wrong: "About 4% of adults meet criteria for ADHD."
  - Right: "About 4% of adults meet criteria for ADHD (Author, Year)."
- **Common knowledge needs no citation.** Don't flag it. **[J]** about where the line falls (e.g., "people differ in how quickly they learn" needs none).
- **Overcitation:** flag the same citation repeated in consecutive sentences when the source and topic have not changed. Cite once, at first relevance in the paragraph.
  - Wrong: "Rivera (2021) argued A (Rivera, 2021). She also noted B (Rivera, 2021)."
  - Right: "Rivera (2021) argued A. She also noted B."
- Flag **citation dumps**: long strings of five or more sources for a routine point. One or two representative sources per key point are typical. Literature reviews are the exception and may be exhaustive. **[J]**
- Flag **sources the writer clearly hasn't read**, such as an attribution that contradicts the cited work or a detail that only a secondary summary would give. Cite only works you have read. **[J]**

### 8.2 Plagiarism (pp. 254–256)

- **Credit is required** whenever the writer paraphrases someone's idea, quotes their words, uses their data or data set, or reproduces or adapts a table, figure, or image. This includes free and Creative Commons images.
- Flag **an uncredited theory, model, or study design.** Ideas need credit, not just words.
  - Wrong: "Anxiety is maintained by avoidance that blocks corrective learning."
  - Right: "... corrective learning, a core claim of emotional processing theory (Foa & Kozak, 1986)."
- Flag **patchwriting**: the source's sentence kept intact with a few words swapped for synonyms, even when it is cited. It reads as a near-copy. Recommend a true paraphrase or a quotation.
  - Source idea (invented): "Clinicians frequently overlook sleep problems in depressed adolescents."
  - Patchwritten: "Practitioners often miss sleep difficulties in depressed teens (Author, Year)."
  - Paraphrased: "Sleep is an under-assessed target in adolescent depression (Author, Year)."
- **Minor citation errors are usually not plagiarism** (a misspelled name, a mistyped year, a missing reference entry). Still flag them as errors, but don't call them plagiarism. **[J]**
- Flag **fabricated or untraceable citations**: a reference that cannot be found or that doesn't contain the claim attributed to it. This is unethical. Verify any citation that looks suspicious. Especially relevant for AI-assisted drafts.
- **Software matches need a human read.** A similarity-checker match is not proof of plagiarism. **[J]**

### 8.3 Self-plagiarism (pp. 256–257)

- Flag **substantial text recycled from the writer's own earlier paper** with no citation, or a whole paper resubmitted for a new class without permission.
- **Brief reuse is acceptable without citation**, for example a standard description of a measure or an analysis method. When reuse is extensive, cite the earlier work and keep the reused text in one or a few paragraphs, introduced with a phrase like "as I described previously." **[J]** about the threshold.
- Flag **quotation marks or block quotes around the writer's own recycled text.** The manual says not to use them; a citation is enough.

### 8.6 Primary and secondary sources (p. 258)

- Flag **an overuse of "as cited in."** Secondary citation is for originals that are unavailable, out of print, or in a language the writer can't read. **[J]**
- Flag **a textbook, lecture, or encyclopedia cited for an original study's finding.** Recommend finding and citing the original, unless the instructor said otherwise.
  - Wrong: "Working memory has limited capacity (Gregory, 2014)." [a textbook summarizing Baddeley]
  - Right: "... (Baddeley, 1986)", after reading Baddeley.

### 8.9 Personal communications (pp. 260–261)

- **Use only for unrecoverable sources**: emails, texts, DMs, phone calls, live talks, unrecorded lectures, and the like.
- Flag **a lecture cited as a personal communication when its content is published elsewhere.** Cite the underlying research instead. Cite the lecture only for original, unpublished content. **[J]**
- Flag **a personal communication used where a recoverable source exists**, such as a recorded lecture or posted slides (those are classroom resources; see 8.8).
- Flag **the writer's own research participants cited as personal communications.** Participant quotes need no citation (see 8.36).
- **Courtesy, not an APA rule:** consider whether the person agreed to be cited. **[J]**

### 8.11 Narrative vs. parenthetical citations as a style choice (p. 263)

- Both formats are correct. The choice is about emphasis:
  - **Narrative** ("Rivera (2024) argued ...") puts the author in the foreground. Use it when who said it matters: a debate, a contrast between authors, or a landmark figure.
  - **Parenthetical** ("... (Rivera, 2024)") puts the idea in the foreground. Use it when the finding matters more than who found it.
- Flag **a paper that opens nearly every sentence with "Author (Year) stated/found ..."**: a source-by-source march that buries the writer's own synthesis. Recommend leading with the idea. **[J]**
  - Wrong: "Kim (2020) found that ... Patel (2021) found that ... Diaz (2022) found that ..."
  - Right: "Stigma predicts delayed help-seeking across three samples (Diaz, 2022; Kim, 2020; Patel, 2021)."
- Flag **an unvaried reporting verb** (said, said, said) and **reporting verbs that misstate certainty**: "proved" for a correlational finding, or "claimed" used to cast doubt. **[J]**
- Flag **dropped-in quotations**: a quotation standing alone as a sentence with no lead-in or follow-up explaining its point. **[J]**

### 8.23 Principles of paraphrasing (p. 269)

- **Paraphrase is the default.** Published authors mostly paraphrase, and students should too. Flag papers whose evidence is mostly direct quotation. **[J]**
- A paraphrase must change **both wording and sentence structure**, and it must still carry a citation. See patchwriting under 8.2.
- Flag **casual or slangy restatement.** A paraphrase keeps a professional tone (see 4.7).
  - Wrong: "Basically, the researchers figured out that therapy kind of works."
  - Right: "The researchers found a moderate effect of therapy on symptom reduction."
- **Page numbers in a paraphrase are optional.** Suggest one when it would help a reader find the passage in a long book. **[J]**
- Flag **a paraphrase of a source the writer met only secondhand** (a paraphrase within another article) and cited as if read directly. Recommend reading the primary source or using "as cited in."

### 8.24 Long paraphrases (pp. 269–270)

- **A multi-sentence paraphrase of one source needs one citation at first mention.** Don't repeat it while context keeps the source clear (overcitation; see 8.1).
- Flag **a paraphrase that continues into a new paragraph without the citation reintroduced.**
- Flag **ambiguous attribution**: a paragraph that switches between sources, or mixes a source with the writer's own claims, without citations marking each switch.
  - Wrong: "Burnout is common among play therapists (Elwood, 2015). Supervision reduces it." [Whose claim is the second sentence?]
  - Right: "... (Elwood, 2015). Regular supervision appears protective (Lee, 2019)." Or mark the second sentence as the writer's own inference.
- Flag **a run of uncited sentences after a cited one where it's unclear whether they're still the source's idea or the writer's view.** **[J]**

### 8.25 When to quote vs. paraphrase (p. 270)

- Quote directly only when:
  1. the writer is **reproducing an exact definition**,
  2. the author put something **memorably or with unusual economy**, or
  3. the writer is **responding to the exact wording**.
- Flag **quotations of ordinary, unremarkable sentences** that should be paraphrased. **[J]**
  - Wrong: Participants "completed the survey online" (Author, Year, p. 4).
  - Right: Participants completed the survey online (Author, Year).
- Flag **quotation-heavy papers.** Instructors may cap quoted material, so check the rubric. **[J]**
- Flag **a quotation with no page or locator.** It is required for quotes, unlike paraphrases. (Locator format is covered elsewhere in the skill.)

### 6.22 Italics for emphasis (pp. 170–172)

- Flag **italics used for mere emphasis.** Italicize for emphasis only when the meaning would otherwise be lost. Otherwise, let the sentence structure carry the stress.
  - Wrong: "This is *not* a stage theory."
  - Right: "This model does not propose developmental stages."
- **Bold, underline, or all-caps used for emphasis in body text:** flag the same way. **[J]**: the manual addresses italics, but the same principle applies.

### 6.24–6.25 Abbreviations (pp. 172–174)

- Flag **abbreviation overload**: several abbreviations packed into one sentence that the reader must decode.
  - Wrong: "At FU, the TAU group showed smaller PA gains and more NA than the MI group."
  - Right: "At follow-up, the treatment-as-usual group showed smaller gains in positive affect, and more negative affect, than the motivational interviewing group."
- Flag **an abbreviation used fewer than three times.** Write the term out instead, unless it is a standard abbreviation for a long, familiar term. **[J]**
- Flag **invented abbreviations for group or condition names** (e.g., "HA group," "LA group"). Spell them out. Don't use S, E, or O for subject, experimenter, or observer.
- Flag **an abbreviation used before it is defined**, or **one that is defined but then alternates** with the spelled-out form. After definition, use only the abbreviation.
  - Wrong: "... cognitive behavioral therapy (CBT) ... later, cognitive behavioral therapy was ..."
- Flag **dictionary-word abbreviations that are defined anyway** (IQ, AIDS). These need no definition, and neither do units, time, Latin, or most statistical abbreviations.
- Flag **an abbreviation defined in a heading.** Define it at the first use in the text instead.

## B. Continuity, tone, conciseness, precision

Source: APA Publication Manual, 7th ed., pp. 111–117 (PDF pp. 135–141). Paraphrased; all examples are original.
Tags: **[hard]** = the manual states it as a rule; flag every instance. **[judgment]** = a guideline; flag only when it hurts clarity, and phrase the finding as a suggestion.
Note: the manual's numbering differs from common summaries. 4.5 is Wordiness and Redundancy; 4.11 is Anthropomorphism. The "strategies to improve writing" (outline, reread, peer help, revise) are §4.25–4.30, outside this file.

### 4.1 Continuity and Flow
- **[judgment]** Flag contradictions, omissions, or irrelevant asides that break the line of argument from opening to conclusion.
  - Wrong: Intro argues stigma lowers help-seeking; Discussion never returns to stigma. → Right: Discussion states what the results imply for the stigma argument.
- **[judgment]** Flag ideas presented out of logical order, or relationships between ideas left unstated.
  - Wrong: "Anxiety rose. Sleep fell." → Right: "As sleep fell, anxiety rose."
- **[judgment]** Flag the same concept named differently across sections (see also 4.4 consistency).

### 4.2 Transitions
- **[judgment]** Flag choppy or abrupt jumps between sentences or paragraphs; often a sign an argument was dropped too early and needs development.
  - Wrong: "Group A improved. Attrition was 12%." → Right: "Group A improved; however, attrition was 12%, which limits that conclusion."
- **[judgment]** Flag missing or mismatched link words. Four families: time (then, after), cause-effect (therefore, as a result), addition (moreover, similarly), contrast (however, although).
  - Wrong: "Scores rose. Therefore, the sample was small." → Right: "Scores rose. However, the sample was small."
- **[hard]** Flag pronouns whose referent in the prior sentence is unclear.
  - Wrong: "Clinicians gave clients the scale, and they found it long." → Right: "... and the clients found it long."
- **[judgment]** Flag overuse or underuse of a single punctuation mark (e.g., a dash in every sentence, or long sentences with no commas).
- **[judgment]** Flag introductory adverbs that add nothing ("interestingly," "importantly," "fortunately," "certainly"); usually cut or recast.
  - Wrong: "Interestingly, women scored higher." → Right: "Women scored higher."

### 4.3 Noun Strings
- **[judgment]** Flag three or more nouns stacked before a final noun. Fix by moving the head noun forward and adding verbs or prepositions; hyphens help only sometimes.
  - Wrong: "adolescent depression screening tool validation study" → Right: "a study validating a tool that screens adolescents for depression"
  - Wrong: "parent training program attendance rates" → Right: "rates of attendance at the parent training program"

### 4.4 Conciseness and Clarity
- **[judgment]** Flag padding added to reach length: elaborations of the obvious, asides, over-detailed apparatus or procedure descriptions beyond reporting standards.
  - Wrong: "It is a well-known fact that sleep is important for people." → Right: (cut, or state the specific claim with a citation)
- **[judgment]** Flag evasiveness, circumlocution, and heavy passive voice that obscure who did what.
  - Wrong: "It was decided that the data would be excluded." → Right: "We excluded the data."
- **[hard]** Flag technical terms a cross-disciplinary reader would not know and that are not defined (see 4.9).
- **[hard]** Flag informal stand-ins for precise verbs, especially "feel" meaning think or believe.
  - Wrong: "We feel the measure is valid." → Right: "We believe the measure is valid." / "The evidence indicates ..."
- **[hard]** Flag words with a statistical meaning used loosely, above all "significant" for "important."
  - Wrong: "a significant concern for clinicians" → Right: "an important concern for clinicians"
- **[hard]** Flag inconsistent labels for the same group, condition, or variable. Pick one and keep it.
  - Wrong: "the waitlist group" ... later "the delayed-treatment participants" → Right: "the waitlist group" throughout
- **[judgment]** Flag synonyms swapped in for variety when they could imply a different thing.
  - Wrong: "stress" then "distress" then "strain" for one construct → Right: "stress" throughout
- **[hard]** Flag biased, pejorative, or outdated group descriptors; defer to the bias-free language checklist (Ch. 5).
- **[judgment]** Flag heavy alliteration, rhyme, poetic flourishes, and clichés.
  - Wrong: "a perfect storm of risk factors" → Right: "several co-occurring risk factors"
- **[hard]** Flag mixed metaphors.
  - Wrong: "this finding opens a new chapter on fertile ground" → Right: "this finding suggests a new line of research"
- **[judgment]** Flag words with surplus or unintended meanings.
  - Wrong: "the outstanding participants were contacted" (meaning remaining) → Right: "the remaining participants were contacted"
- **[judgment]** Flag figurative or colorful expressions that may not travel across cultures or readers.

### 4.5 Wordiness and Redundancy
- **[judgment]** Flag wordy phrases with a shorter equivalent.
  - "due to the fact that" → "because"; "in order to" → "to"; "at this point in time" → "now"
  - Wrong: "There were 40 clients who dropped out." → Right: "Forty clients dropped out."
- **[hard]** Flag redundancy (two words that say the same thing); delete the extra word.
  - "past history" → "history"; "end result" → "result"; "each and every" → "each"; "true facts" → "facts"
  - "brief in duration" → "brief"; "previously reported before" → "previously reported"; "both equally similar" → "similar"
  - "the reason is because" → "the reason is that"; "three distinct groups" (when distinctness is not the point) → "three groups"
- **[judgment]** Flag redundancy used for emphasis; suggest getting emphasis from position (start or end of sentence) instead.
  - Wrong: "This is a really very critical finding." → Right: "Most critical was the finding that ..."
- **[judgment]** Flag passive constructions where active voice would be shorter (see §4.13 in Ch. 4b).
- **[judgment]** Flag embellished or flowery prose.

### 4.6 Sentence and Paragraph Length
- **[hard]** Do NOT flag a sentence for length alone; APA sets no minimum or maximum.
- **[judgment]** Flag runs of short, simple sentences that read as choppy.
  - Wrong: "We recruited adults. They were from clinics. They completed surveys." → Right: "We recruited adults from clinics, who then completed surveys."
- **[judgment]** Flag sentences carrying more than one main idea; suggest splitting.
  - Wrong: "The PHQ-9 was used because it is brief, and scores were higher in winter, which may reflect seasonal effects that prior work has also noted." → Right: split into a method sentence and a result sentence.
- **[judgment]** Flag long sentences whose parts do not proceed in a logical order.
- **[judgment]** Flag one-sentence paragraphs (acceptable only occasionally).
- **[judgment]** Flag paragraphs longer than about one double-spaced page; suggest a break at a shift in idea.

### 4.7 Tone
- **[judgment]** Flag creative-writing devices: deliberate ambiguity, suspense, withheld information, sudden shifts in topic, tense, or person.
  - Wrong: "But what the team found next surprised everyone." → Right: "Contrary to the hypothesis, scores decreased."
- **[judgment]** Flag wording that draws attention to sound or cleverness over content.
- **[hard]** Flag combative or dismissive descriptions of other researchers; contrast positions neutrally.
  - Wrong: "Lee (2021) completely ignored attrition." → Right: "Lee (2021) did not report attrition."
  - Wrong: "Smith's flawed and naive model" → Right: "Smith's model does not account for ..."
- **[judgment]** Flag prose that is flat or listless where elaboration would help; the target is direct but engaging, written for an informed reader outside the subfield.

### 4.8 Contractions and Colloquialisms
- **[hard]** Flag contractions in the author's own prose.
  - Wrong: "Results don't support H2." → Right: "Results do not support H2."
- **[hard]** Exceptions; do NOT flag: contractions inside direct quotations (including participant speech), contractions discussed as language examples, and idioms quoted as such.
- **[hard]** Flag "it's" used as a possessive; possessive pronouns take no apostrophe.
  - Wrong: "The scale and it's subscales" → Right: "The scale and its subscales"
- **[hard]** Flag colloquialisms and slang.
  - Wrong: "We wrote up the findings" / "a lot of clients" / "kids" → Right: "We reported the findings" / "many clients" / "children"
- **[hard]** Flag vague quantity approximations in empirical statements; replace with numbers.
  - Wrong: "almost all participants" / "very few dropped out" → Right: "94% of participants" / "three participants (4%) dropped out"

### 4.9 Jargon
- **[hard]** Flag specialized terms key to the paper that are not defined at first use.
  - Wrong: "Scores showed ceiling effects." (no definition, general audience) → Right: "Scores clustered near the maximum (a ceiling effect), ..."
- **[judgment]** Flag dense jargon even where the vocabulary is relevant; aim for readers outside the specialty.
- **[hard]** Flag euphemistic jargon that replaces a plain term.
  - Wrong: "negative patient care outcome" → Right: "patient death"
  - Wrong: "workforce rightsizing" → Right: "layoffs"
- **[judgment]** Flag bureaucratic or academic filler terms ("operationalize the leveraging of synergies").

### 4.10 Logical Comparisons
- **[hard]** Flag comparisons that are ambiguous because a key word is missing.
  - Wrong: "Therapists consulted supervisors more than peers." → Right: "Therapists consulted supervisors more than they consulted peers." / "... more than peers did."
- **[hard]** Flag comparisons of nonparallel things (an attribute vs. a group).
  - Wrong: "The scores of veterans were higher than civilians." → Right: "The scores of veterans were higher than those of civilians."
  - Wrong: "Our effect size was larger than Kim (2020)." → Right: "Our effect size was larger than Kim's (2020)."
- **[judgment]** Flag incomplete comparisons with no stated referent.
  - Wrong: "The new protocol was more effective." → Right: "The new protocol was more effective than usual care."

### 4.11 Anthropomorphism
- **[hard]** Flag human traits or actions attributed to animals that imply a misleading human parallel.
  - Wrong: "the mouse mothers grieved" → Right: "the dams reduced activity after pup removal"
- **[hard]** Flag inanimate things (theories, tables, studies) doing what only people do: conclude, believe, feel, decide, interpret, try.
  - Wrong: "The theory concludes that ..." → Right: "The theory addresses ..." / "Proponents of the theory conclude ..."
  - Wrong: "Table 2 believes ..." / "The study tried to ..." → Right: "Table 2 shows ..." / "We tried to ..."
- **[judgment]** Prefer human actors with active verbs for actions researchers performed.
  - Wrong: "The study extrapolated the trend." → Right: "We extrapolated the trend."
- **[hard]** Do NOT flag accepted constructions: "this section addresses," "the chapter focuses on," "the results suggest," "the data provide," "the study found," "the research contributes."
- **[judgment]** Borderline cases: decide by whether the phrasing misleads or slows the reader; otherwise leave it.

## C. Grammar and usage

Source: *Publication Manual* (7th ed.), pp. 117–127 (PDF pp. 141–151). All wording is paraphrased; all examples are original.
Tags: **[HARD]** = APA states a firm rule; **[JUDGMENT]** = depends on context or emphasis; flag it as a suggestion, not an error.
4.25–4.29 are writing-process advice (read models, outline, reread, peer review, writing centers). There is nothing to audit there.

**Verbs**

### 4.12 Verb tense
- **[HARD] Past for a definite past event.** Flag present tense used for a specific completed study or event.
  - Wrong: "Lee (2019) argues that sleep loss impairs recall." → Right: "Lee (2019) argued that ..."
- **[HARD] Present perfect for an indefinite past or an action that continues into the present.**
  - Wrong: "Since 2010, researchers studied this effect widely." → Right: "Since 2010, researchers have studied ..."
- **[JUDGMENT] Unnecessary tense shifts.** Flag tense changes inside a paragraph, or between adjacent paragraphs, that do not reflect a real change in time.
  - Wrong: "Participants sorted the cards. Then they rate each one." → Right: "... Then they rated each one."
- **[HARD] Tense matches the paper section.** Use the table below.

| Paper section | Recommended tense | Original example |
|---|---|---|
| Literature review / others' work | Past | "Okafor (2021) reported ..." |
| | Present perfect | "Several teams have replicated ..." |
| Method / procedure | Past | "Raters coded each transcript." |
| | Present perfect | "Prior trials have used this scale." |
| Reporting results | Past | "Anxiety scores declined." / "The effect was not significant." |
| Discussion of implications | Present | "These findings suggest ..." |
| Conclusions, limitations, future directions | Present | "We conclude ..." / "One limitation is ..." |

- Audit note: present tense in the Results ("scores decline") and past tense for implications ("these findings suggested") are both common slips. Flag them.

### 4.13 Active and passive voice
- **[JUDGMENT] Prefer active voice.** Flag passive voice where the actor matters, especially for what participants or researchers did.
  - Wrong: "The questionnaire was completed by the clients." → Right: "The clients completed the questionnaire."
- **[JUDGMENT] Passive is acceptable when the object is the point.** Do not flag passive that rightly puts the focus on the thing acted on (for example, equipment setup).
  - Fine: "The electrodes were placed at Fz and Cz."
- **[HARD-ish] Flag an agentless passive that hides who acted** when the reader needs to know.
  - Wrong: "Exclusion decisions were made." → Right: "Two independent raters made exclusion decisions."

### 4.14 Mood
- **[HARD] Indicative for factual statements.**
- **[HARD] Subjunctive only for conditions that are contrary to fact or improbable.** Flag "was" in an unreal condition, and flag subjunctive used for ordinary conditions.
  - Wrong: "If the sample was more diverse, we could generalize." → Right: "If the sample were more diverse, ..."
- **[HARD] No hedging with "would."** Flag "it would seem," "it would appear," "this would suggest."
  - Wrong: "It would appear that stigma reduces help-seeking." → Right: "Stigma appears to reduce help-seeking."
  - Fine: habitual "would" ("The client would pace during sessions") and conditional "would" ("We would extend the study if funded").

### 4.15 Subject–verb agreement
- **[HARD] The verb agrees with the true subject, ignoring intervening phrases** ("as well as," "together with," "including," "plus").
  - Wrong: "The accuracy of the raters, along with their speed, improve over time." → Right: "... improves over time."
- **[JUDGMENT] Collective nouns** (set, pair, faculty, series, number). Use singular when the group acts as a unit and plural when its members act individually. Flag only clear mismatches.
  - Unit: "The number of referrals is rising." Individuals: "A number of clinicians are reluctant."
- **[HARD] "None"** takes a singular verb with a singular noun and a plural verb with a plural noun.
  - Wrong: "None of the data was missing." → Right: "None of the data were missing." ("Data" is plural in APA.) / "None of the evidence was ..."
- **[HARD] "Or"/"nor" compounds** agree with the nearer noun.
  - Wrong: "Either the therapists or the supervisor review the tape." → Right: "... the supervisor reviews the tape."

**Pronouns**

### 4.16 First- versus third-person pronouns
- **[HARD] Use first person for your own actions and views.** Flag "the author," "the researcher," or "the present writer" used to mean yourself.
  - Wrong: "The researcher administered the scale." → Right: "I administered the scale."
- **[HARD] A sole author uses "I," never "we."** Flag "we" in a single-author paper (most student papers).
  - Wrong (sole author): "We argue that ..." → Right: "I argue that ..."
- **[HARD] Coauthors use "we."** Use the third person by name only for one coauthor's specific contribution.
  - Fine: "We collected the data; Priya Shah conducted all follow-up calls."
- Note: names of coauthors in the text may need masking for masked review.

### 4.17 Editorial "we"
- **[HARD] Flag "we," "us," or "our" meaning people in general or a vague group.** Replace it with a specific noun.
  - Wrong: "We often misjudge our own emotions." → Right: "People often misjudge their own emotions."
- **[JUDGMENT] "We" is fine once the group is named.**
  - Fine: "As clinicians, we tend to overestimate rapport."

### 4.18 Singular "they"
- **[HARD] Use "they" for anyone who uses "they" as their pronoun.**
- **[HARD] Use "they" as the generic pronoun when gender is unknown or irrelevant.** Flag generic "he" or "she" used alone.
  - Wrong: "Each client rated his satisfaction." → Right: "Each client rated their satisfaction."
- **[HARD] Flag "(s)he," "s/he," and alternating he/she**, unless the person uses those forms.
- **[JUDGMENT] Use "he or she" sparingly**, and only when it matches the people described.
- Forms: they, them, their, theirs, themselves. "Themself" is also acceptable for a single referent. Do not flag either one.
- **[JUDGMENT] If "they" reads awkwardly, suggest a rewrite**: rephrase, pluralize, swap the pronoun for an article ("the"), or drop the pronoun.
  - Example: "A trainee should submit his log weekly." → "Trainees should submit their logs weekly." / "A trainee should submit the log weekly."

### 4.19 "Who" versus "that" (people vs. animals and things)
- **[HARD] "Who" for humans; "that" or "which" for animals and objects.**
  - Wrong: "Clients that dropped out were contacted." → Right: "Clients who dropped out ..."
- **[JUDGMENT] Animals take "it,"** but a named animal of known sex may take a gendered pronoun.

### 4.20 "Who" versus "whom"
- **[HARD] "Who" is a subject; "whom" is the object of a verb or preposition.** Test it by substitution: if he/she/they fits, use "who"; if him/her/them fits, use "whom."
  - Wrong: "Adolescents whom reported self-harm were referred." → Right: "Adolescents who reported ..." (they reported)
  - Wrong: "Parents, each of who signed consent, ..." → Right: "... each of whom signed consent" (of them)

### 4.21 Restrictive vs. nonrestrictive clauses ("that" vs. "which")
- **[HARD in APA] "That" introduces restrictive clauses, with no commas.** Flag "which" without commas used to restrict.
  - Wrong: "Items which loaded below .40 were dropped." → Right: "Items that loaded below .40 were dropped."
- **[HARD] "Which" introduces nonrestrictive clauses, set off with commas.**
  - Wrong: "The clinic that serves rural counties was closed in 2020." (when there is only one clinic) → Right: "The clinic, which serves rural counties, ..."
- Audit tip: a comma before "that" in a relative clause, or a bare "which" with no comma, is a likely error.

**Sentence construction**

### 4.22 Subordinate conjunctions
- **[JUDGMENT, strongly preferred] Use "while" only for simultaneous events.** Otherwise use "although," "whereas," "despite," "and," or "but."
  - Wrong: "While men reported more anger, women reported more worry." → Right: "Whereas men reported ..., women ..."
  - Fine: "Participants listened to tones while tracking a dot."
- **[JUDGMENT] Replace "since" with "because" when it could be read as temporal.** Leave it when the meaning is unambiguous.
  - Wrong: "Scores dropped since the protocol changed." → Right: "Scores dropped because the protocol changed." (if causal)

### 4.23 Misplaced and dangling modifiers
- **[HARD] Misplaced modifier.** Flag modifiers placed where they attach to the wrong word. Move them next to their target.
  - Wrong: "Using a checklist, the supervisor observed the trainees." (who used it?) → Right: "The supervisor observed the trainees, who used a checklist."
- **[HARD] "Based on" must modify a noun.** Flag an introductory "Based on ..." followed by a person or other subject it cannot describe. Prefer "On the basis of."
  - Wrong: "Based on these scores, we diagnosed ..." → Right: "On the basis of these scores, we ..."
- **[HARD] "Only" goes next to what it limits.**
  - Wrong: "The scale only measures one facet." → Right: "The scale measures only one facet."
- **[HARD] Squinting modifier.** Flag a modifier that could attach to the words before it or after it.
  - Wrong: "Clients who exercise often sleep better." → Right: "Clients who often exercise sleep better." / "Clients who exercise sleep better often."
- **[HARD] Dangling modifier.** Flag an introductory phrase with no logical subject in the sentence. These often come from passive voice; rewriting in active voice usually fixes them.
  - Wrong: "After coding the interviews, themes were identified." → Right: "After coding the interviews, we identified themes."
  - Wrong: "Consistent with prior work, Diaz (2022) found higher dropout." → Right: "Diaz (2022) found higher dropout, a result consistent with prior work."

### 4.24 Parallel construction
- **[HARD] Repeat the parallel marker.** If the first element opens with "that," the second does too. Use no comma before the conjunction when there are only two parts.
  - Wrong: "We found that stress rose, and burnout increased." → Right: "We found that stress rose and that burnout increased."
- **[HARD] Paired conjunctions** (between/and, both/and, either/or, neither/nor, not only/but also): put the first word directly before the first parallel element, and make both halves match.
  - Wrong: "They either completed the CBT module or the ACT module." → Right: "They completed either the CBT module or the ACT module."
  - Wrong: "It is not only costly but also that it is slow." → Right: "It is not only costly but also slow."
- **[HARD] "Between ... and," never "between ... –".**
  - Wrong: "between 18–25 years old" → Right: "between 18 and 25 years old"
- **[HARD] Never use "both ... as well as."** (It is redundant.)
  - Wrong: "both reliable as well as valid" → Right: "both reliable and valid"
- **[HARD] Series and lists are parallel in form**, including bulleted lists and table stubs.
  - Wrong: "Clients were asked to arrive early, to bring ID, and that they should fast." → Right: "... to arrive early, to bring ID, and to fast."

**Strategies**

### 4.30 Revising a paper (auditor's scope note)
- Grammar and usage are checked at the detail level against 4.12–4.24, after the big-picture checks: a clear thesis, logical organization, and fit to the rubric or journal.
- The spelling authorities are Merriam-Webster and the *APA Dictionary of Psychology*.
- Grammar checkers miss things. Do not treat an automated "pass" as clearance.
- For grammar questions the manual does not cover, defer to a standard grammar reference rather than inventing an APA rule.

## D. Bias-free language

Source: *Publication Manual* (7th ed., 2020), Ch. 5, §5.1–5.10, printed pp. 131–149 (PDF pp. 155–173).
Everything is paraphrased and verified against the manual text; examples are invented unless noted.
**[judgment]** = raise as a suggestion, not an error. Overriding rule throughout the chapter: the
terms people use for themselves outrank any table here (except stigmatizing terms; see 5.2, 5.3).

**General principles**

### 5.1 Describe at the appropriate level of specificity
- Flag characteristics mentioned when irrelevant to the research question (e.g. sexual orientation in a cognition study). **[judgment]**
- Flag relevant differences left unreported, especially how the sample differs from the population the author generalizes to.
- When unsure, prefer the more specific term; aggregated data can't be split later.
- Flag vague descriptors where the manual's specific alternative exists:

| Topic | Vague | More specific |
|---|---|---|
| Age | "over 65" | "aged 65–80", plus mean and median |
| Disability | "people with dementia" | "people with Alzheimer's disease" |
| Gender | "women" (when it matters) | "cisgender women", "transgender women" |
| Research role | "people" | "participants", "patients", "clients" |
| Race/ethnicity | "Asian Americans" | "Chinese Americans" |
| Sexual orientation | "gay" (umbrella) | "lesbians, gay men, bisexual people" |
| SES | "low income" | "below the federal poverty threshold for a family of four" |

- If several characteristics are relevant, check that their intersection is addressed (see 5.10).

### 5.2 Be sensitive to labels
- Flag labels the group doesn't use for itself; call people what they call themselves. Where usage is debated, the author should show they checked (asked participants or consulted self-advocacy groups). **[judgment]**
- Flag repeating slurs or stigmatizing terms just because participants used them about themselves.
- Flag adjectives used as nouns and labels that equate people with a condition:

| Flag | Prefer |
|---|---|
| the poor | people living in poverty |
| the gays | gay men, gay people |
| schizophrenics, amnesiacs | people with schizophrenia / amnesia |
| the learning disabled | people with learning disabilities |
| drug users | people who use drugs |

- Allow capitalized community labels the group chose (e.g. Deaf), even adjectival ones; not everyone with hearing loss identifies as Deaf.
- **Operational labels:** after defining a group by a measure, refer to it by that measure ("the high verbal group"). Flag cryptic abbreviations ("LDs", "HVAs"); "Group A" is acceptable but uninformative.
- **False hierarchies:** flag one group used as the unexplained standard, and "normal" comparison groups ("lesbians vs. normal women" → "lesbians vs. straight women").
- Flag nonparallel group names (see 5.7).
- **Order:** flag dominant groups listed first by default ("men and women", "White Americans and racial minorities", dominant groups at the left of a graph or top of a table). Fix: alphabetical or sample-size order, kept consistent throughout. **[judgment]**

**Reducing bias by topic**

### 5.3 Age
- Flag missing ages in Method; check for range, mean and median.
- Flag open-ended bands ("under 18", "over 65") unless they're eligibility criteria.
- Check age-group nouns:

| Age | Terms |
|---|---|
| any age | person, individual |
| 12 and younger | infant (very young), child, girl, boy, transgender girl/boy, gender-fluid child |
| 13–17 | adolescent, young person, youth, young woman/man, female/male adolescent |
| 18+ | adult, woman, man, trans man/woman, genderqueer adult, cisgender adult |

- Flag "males"/"females" as nouns unless the group spans a broad age range (see 5.5).
- **Older adults:**

| Flag | Prefer |
|---|---|
| seniors, the elderly, the aged, aging dependents | older adults, older people, older persons, older patients, persons 65 years and older, the older population |
| senile, senility | dementia, naming the type when known |
| the young-old | young-old individuals (adjective only, with ages defined) |

- These terms stay out even if participants use them for themselves.
- Flag aging framed as decline or something to overcome, or conflated with disease.
- When older adults are contrasted with another age group, check that the other group is named specifically ("young adults vs. older adults").
- Flag generational labels (boomers, millennials, Gen Z) outside studies of generations. Decade terms (octogenarian) are allowed.

### 5.4 Disability
- **Person-first vs identity-first:** both are acceptable, and so is mixing them, *unless* the group is known to prefer one. Then use theirs. The person's or group's stated preference outranks style.

| Approach | Example | When it fits |
|---|---|---|
| Person-first | person with paraplegia, youth with epilepsy, people with intellectual disabilities | emphasizes the person; avoids "a paraplegic", "an epileptic" |
| Identity-first | autistic person, blind person, amputee, Deaf | people who claim the disability as cultural or personal identity |
| Mixed | either form in one paper | allowed; it avoids repetitive "person with…" |

- If unsure, the author should follow self-advocacy groups, or the participants' own words. **[judgment]**
- Flag negative, restrictive or slur terms:

| Flag | Prefer |
|---|---|
| wheelchair-bound, confined to a wheelchair | wheelchair user |
| AIDS victim | person with AIDS |
| brain damaged | person with a traumatic brain injury |
| cripple, invalid | person with a physical disability |
| nuts | person with a mental illness (or the specific diagnosis) |
| alcoholic | person with alcohol use disorder |
| meth addict, substance abuser | person with substance use disorder |
| the mentally retarded | people with intellectual disabilities |
| hearing-impaired | hard of hearing |
| high/low functioning | the person's specific strengths and weaknesses |

- Flag condescending euphemisms ("special needs", "physically challenged", "handi-capable").
- Flag disabilities named when irrelevant, and heterogeneity that goes unmentioned when it matters (injury level, symptom severity).
- Flag deficit-only portrayals; describe capabilities as well as concerns.
- "Patients" fits health care settings; elsewhere use "clients" or another term (see 5.6).

### 5.5 Gender
- **Sex vs gender:** "gender" = social construct and identity; use it for people as social groups. "Sex" = biological sex assignment; use it when that distinction is the point. Flag the two used interchangeably. Discussing both is fine when the factors can't be separated. **[judgment]**
- Flag gender identity conflated with sexual orientation (they're distinct).
- Flag samples assumed cisgender. Check that Method reports gender identities (cisgender, transgender, nonbinary, etc.).
- "Transgender" is an adjective ("transgender people"). TGNC is the accepted umbrella term. Use people's own identity labels and define them.
- Flag "two-spirit" applied outside Indigenous and Native American communities.
- **Sex assignment:**

| Flag | Prefer |
|---|---|
| birth sex, natal sex | assigned sex, sex assigned at birth |
| tranny, transvestite | (never use) |
| transsexual | only for someone who claims it |
| person assigned girl at birth | person assigned female at birth |

- **Names:** use the name the person uses, within confidentiality rules.
- **Nouns:** use specific ones (trans women, cisgender men, gender-fluid people). "Male"/"female" are fine as adjectives. As nouns, only for a broad or ambiguous age range or for sex assigned at birth.
- Flag generic "man"/"mankind" → "people", "individuals". Flag gendered job titles ("policeman" → "police officer", "housewife" → "homemaker"). If a source uses generic "he"/"man", the author should note the historical context.
- **Pronouns:**
  1. **"Preferred pronouns"** → "pronouns" / "identified pronouns".
  2. **Known person:** use their pronouns (they, neopronouns, or their name), whatever their sex assigned at birth.
  3. **Unknown or generic person:** singular "they". Flag generic "he", role stereotypes ("the nurse … she"), and alternating he/she.
  4. **Combined forms:** flag "he/she" and "(s)he" always. Flag "he or she" unless every referent uses those pronouns, and then only sparingly.
- Flag "opposite sex/gender" → "another sex/gender". Couples: "mixed-gender/mixed-sex" or "same-gender/same-sex".

### 5.6 Participation in research
- "Participants" **and** "subjects" are both acceptable, as are descriptive terms ("college students", "respondents"). Don't flag "subjects" as an error. **[judgment]** Suggest "participants" only if the author asks for a preference. Keep it in set terms ("within-subjects design").
- "Patient" = someone receiving health care services for a diagnosed condition. "Client" may suit academic, school or business settings. The recipients' own preference governs.
- Flag cases treated as people: "manic-depressive cases were treated" → "people with bipolar disorder were treated".
- Flag "patient management/placement" → "coordination of care", "supportive services".
- Flag loose "borderline" and "at risk". Check that the score range or diagnosis is specified, and who is at risk of what.
- Flag passive voice that makes participants objects ("the participants were run" → "participants completed…").
- Flag "failed to complete" → "did not complete".

### 5.7 Racial and ethnic identity
- **Race vs ethnicity:** race = physical differences treated as socially significant; ethnicity = shared culture (language, ancestry, practices, beliefs). Flag the two confused, or racial labels imposed on ethnic groups.
- Prefer participants' own terms and the most specific origin known (Japanese American over Asian American; Bolivian over Latino).
- **Capitalization:** capitalize racial/ethnic names: Black, White, Native American, Hispanic, Indigenous, Aboriginal. Flag lowercase "black"/"white" for people. Never use other colors for groups.
- "Indigenous Peoples" is capitalized for a specific group; lowercase "people" in general description ("the authors were Indigenous people").
- "Multiracial", "biracial", "multiethnic" are lowercase; the group names inside stay capitalized.
- **No hyphens:** "Asian-American participants" → "Asian American participants".
- **Group terms:**

| Flag | Prefer / note |
|---|---|
| Negro, Afro-American | Black or African American (both acceptable in the U.S.) |
| African American for all of the diaspora | Black, or specific nationality (Nigerian, Jamaican) |
| Asian (meaning U.S. people) | Asian American / Asian Canadian; "Asian" = people in Asia |
| Oriental (people) | Asian, Asian American, or region/nation |
| Caucasian | White, European American (or European Australian, etc.), or nation |
| tribe | people, nation; specific name (Cherokee, Navajo) |
| American Indian | Native American / Native North American acceptable and may be preferred; "Indian" usually means people from India |
| Eskimo | Inuit, Iñupiat, Alaska Natives, or specific nation |
| Maori | Māori (with macron) |
| Middle Eastern (vague) | nation of origin; "Arab Americans" for U.S. residents claiming Arab ancestry |
| Hispanic vs Latino | not interchangeable; use the participants' term. Latino/a, Latin@, Latinx are all acceptable; nation of origin preferred |

- **Parallel designations:** flag pairs that mix color and origin ("African Americans and Whites" → "Blacks and Whites" or "African Americans and European Americans").
- **Essentialism:** flag "the Black race", "the White race".
- **"Minorities":** flag it as a bare collective → "people of color", "underrepresented groups", or the specific groups. If needed, use it with a modifier ("racial minority", "ethnic minority").
- Flag minority groups assumed to be underprivileged. Alternatives: "economically marginalized", or a specific description (e.g. underfunded schools that are majority Black).

### 5.8 Sexual orientation
- Flag "sexual preference", "sexual identity", "sexual orientation identity" → "sexual orientation". The orientation itself isn't chosen.
- Allow orientation terms: lesbian, gay, heterosexual, straight, asexual, demisexual, bisexual, queer, pansexual, polysexual. Prefer self-identification.
- Flag "homosexual"/"homosexuality" → specific identity-first terms (gay men, bisexual people).
- Flag "gays" → "gay men" / "gay people". "Lesbians" = "lesbian women", acceptable. Flag undefined "gay" where scope is unclear.
- "Straight" and "heterosexual" are both acceptable.
- **Umbrella terms:** "sexual and gender minorities", "sexual orientation and gender diversity". LGBTQ, LGBTQ+ and LGBTQIA(+) are acceptable but must be defined and must match the groups described. Flag "LGBT" as outdated.
- Flag an abbreviation used where one specific group is meant; when in doubt, use an umbrella term.
- Flag sexual-minority comparison groups called "normal" or "the general public" (see 5.2).

### 5.9 Socioeconomic status
- Flag SES missing from Method, or reported without detail. Check for:
  1. **Income:** adjusted for household size or tied to federal poverty guidelines.
  2. **Education.**
  3. **Occupation or employment.**
  4. **Context:** housing (renting, owning, subsidized) and neighborhood indicators (median income, free-lunch share).
- Flag pejorative terms:

| Flag | Prefer |
|---|---|
| the homeless | people experiencing homelessness, people in emergency shelter, people in transitional housing |
| welfare mothers, welfare reliant | mothers who receive TANF benefits |
| inner-city, ghetto, the projects, poverty stricken | specific neighborhood or income description |
| high school dropouts, poorly educated | people without a high school diploma or equivalent |
| achievement gap | opportunity gap |

- Flag SES terms used as implicit stand-ins for race. Check that racial/ethnic descriptors appear within SES categories ("low-income Puerto Rican fathers").
- Flag deficit language that blames individuals rather than context. **[judgment]**

### 5.10 Intersectionality
- Flag characteristics reported only as separate marginals. Use crossed groups instead: "20 African American women, 15 European American women…", not "35 women; 43 African American".
- Flag results that credit one characteristic when an intersection may explain them.
- Flag identity treated as additive (race + gender + orientation) when the paper's point is a distinct combined experience. **[judgment]**
- Flag analyses that ignore privilege. People can hold privileged and oppressed positions at once, and these vary by context.
- Check that each identity term in a combined description follows its own section (5.3–5.9).

**Quick audit pass**
1. **Search terms:** elderly, seniors, senile, subjects (note only), cases, failed, normal, minorities, non-White, Caucasian, Oriental, tribe, Eskimo, homosexual, gays, preference, opposite sex, he or she, he/she, (s)he, preferred pronouns, mankind, -man titles, wheelchair-bound, victim, suffers, addict, alcoholic, special needs, high/low functioning, the homeless, inner-city, welfare, dropout, achievement gap.
2. **Case and hyphens:** check lowercase black/white for people and hyphenated group names.
3. **Sex/gender wording:** check it against what was actually measured.
4. **Relevance and specificity:** check every demographic mention is relevant and as specific as the data allow.
5. **Reporting:** report each finding as an error or a **[judgment]** suggestion; never rewrite quoted text.

**Beyond the 2020 manual (later APA guidance / common usage — not in Ch. 5)**
These terms appear in later APA style resources or general usage, not in the printed 7th edition.
Treat any flag based on them as **[judgment]** only:
- **SES and immigration:** "unhoused"; "undocumented immigrant" for "illegal alien".
- **Ethnicity:** "Latine".
- **Race:** BIPOC.
- **Disability:** "congenital disability" for "birth defect"; "differently abled" as a euphemism.
- **Gendered words:** "chair" for "chairman"; "workforce" for "manpower".
- **Substance use:** "negative/positive" for "clean/dirty" screens.
- **Sexual orientation:** "lifestyle"; "MSM" used as an identity label.
